# No National Stereotypes — A Binding Worldbuilding Rule

**Status:** Confirmed, binding — applies across every Tepenian Universe project (games, novels, TV)

**The rule:** a Tepenian city's real-world founding nation (which country originally established the underlying Antarctic research station) is a GPS coordinate only. It locates the city on the map. It must never be used as a reason, cause, or explanation for anything else in the fiction — not a city's present-day culture, not a faction's internal conflict, not a character's temperament, not which real-world city makes a good creative inspiration, not why a war strike hit one city and not another.

**Self-check:** if a sentence's meaning would change were the founding nation swapped for a different one, that sentence violates this rule.

**The scale of time that makes this rule necessary, not just a style preference:** the Falkland Treaty was signed **June 21, 2564.** William Shakespeare was born April 23, 1564. **We are, today, closer to the birth of William Shakespeare than we are to the signing of the Falkland Treaty** — and closer to the publication of the King James Bible (1611) too. Expecting a Tepenian city's present-day culture to still resemble its founding nation's real-world stereotypes is exactly as implausible as expecting modern English culture to still resemble Shakespeare's England just because Shakespeare was English. That much elapsed time makes a founding nation pure geography — a GPS coordinate marking where something started, never a live causal force on what a culture looks like on the far side of that gap.

**Sharper still, confirmed 2026-07-24: the founding generation itself had zero living connection, not just present-day Tepenians.** Real-world Antarctic research stations mostly date to the International Geophysical Year (1957-58); even the oldest continuously-run station (Argentina's Orcadas, 1904) is still roughly 600-660 years short of the Falkland Treaty. **The station's real-world national origin was already ancient history on day one of Tepenia's own existence** — the 2564 exiles who actually signed the treaty had no living connection to whichever national team built the station they arrived at, before a single day of independent Tepenian culture had happened yet. **Direct consequence:** a city's own founding-history narrative should never read as if the real-world station's construction and Tepenia's founding happened in causal or temporal proximity — the station sat as inert, centuries-old infrastructure long before any exile ever arrived at it. This rules out more than culture-as-inheritance: it rules out **real station personnel from the 1950s-70s still being alive, present, or personally interacting with the 2564 founding exiles** — a distinct, more literal violation than a stereotype. What the exiles plausibly inherit is the **physical shell only** (a runway, a hangar, a building) — never institutional knowledge, personnel, command structure, or "continuity," since nobody who built or ran the original station survived to hand any of that down. Any inherited operational skill or discipline in a founding-era story should instead be sourced from **ex-program exiles among the founding population itself** — real people with real 20th/21st-century institutional backgrounds, exiled in 2564, who recognize and reclaim derelict infrastructure — never from the original station's own personnel bridging a 600-plus-year gap.

## Every way this has been violated before (and corrected, in InnerTepeniaGDD)

1. Explaining a city's present-day culture or a faction's internal conflict via founding-nation stereotype ("German rigor," "Russian dusha") instead of the city's own independently-developed identity.
2. A bare "Being [Nationality]." or "[Nationality] Tepenian city" identity claim in a summary line.
3. Picking, rejecting, or even neutrally mentioning whether a real-world creative-inspiration pick shares a nationality with a city's founding nation — even a defensive "no overlap, no issue" note is itself a violation.
4. Framing an established in-world cultural practice (an institution, cuisine, music, art) as inherited from or carried over from the founding real-world nation, rather than developed independently by the exile community. A practice may echo a real-world tradition as coincidence; it must never be explained by that tradition.
5. Using a city's founding nation as an explanatory factor in an in-world strategic, military, or narrative decision (e.g. "Upper Earth struck City X, not City Y, because X's founding nation gave it cleaner symbolic legibility as a target").
6. Writing a city's founding-history narrative as if the real-world station's original national personnel, command structure, or institutional continuity survived the 600-plus-year gap to the 2564 exiles — e.g. describing an "Air Force institutional character from the founding era" as if it passed down from the actual 1950s-70s Air Force rather than being independently re-established by ex-Air Force exiles among the 2564 arrivals themselves.

## How to apply

- The founding nation can still appear as a bare fact: demographic tier tables, "the station was founded by X in 19XX," a naming choice honoring a specific historical figure. It just can never be the *reason* something else in the fiction is true.
- When writing a city's culture, institutions, cuisine, music, or art, build it from the exile community's own circumstances (isolation, density, terrain, founding-era pressures) — never from the founding nation's real-world culture.
- When choosing real-world creative inspirations for a city, judge them entirely on structural/thematic parallel — never mention whether they share or don't share a nationality with the city's founding nation, in either direction (even a defensive disclaimer that there's no overlap is itself the mistake).
- When explaining a plot event (why a strike hit one city and not another, why a faction formed where it did), build the explanation from the city's own established facts — population, geography, infrastructure, civic role, timeline — never from its founding nation.
- When writing a city's founding-era history, a real-world station can hand the exiles a **physical shell only** — a standing runway, hangar, or building, dormant and unstaffed for centuries. It can never hand them personnel, institutional knowledge, or command continuity. If a founding story needs someone with real operational expertise, source it from **ex-program exiles among the 2564 founding population** (real people with real prior institutional backgrounds, themselves exiled), not from the original station's own long-dead staff.

## Case study: Juan Carlos

Juan Carlos (Palmer subnet) was founded on a pre-exile Spanish research station. Its own source material once framed its defining civic institution (a "tertulia") and its cuisine/music/arts as directly inherited from Spain — corrected in InnerTepeniaGDD to note any such institution would have to be independently developed by the exile community, with real-world echoes noted only as coincidence, never as cause. This repo's own Juan Carlos entry (`Worldspace/Locations/Juan_Carlos.md`) goes a step further and omits the unconfirmed cultural institution entirely rather than porting it over in reframed form, pending real independent development.
