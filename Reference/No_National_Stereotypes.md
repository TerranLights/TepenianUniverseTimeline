# No National Stereotypes — A Binding Worldbuilding Rule

**Status:** Confirmed, binding — applies across every Tepenian Universe project (games, novels, TV)

**The rule:** a Tepenian city's real-world founding nation (which country originally established the underlying Antarctic research station) is a GPS coordinate only. It locates the city on the map. It must never be used as a reason, cause, or explanation for anything else in the fiction — not a city's present-day culture, not a faction's internal conflict, not a character's temperament, not which real-world city makes a good creative inspiration, not why a war strike hit one city and not another.

**Self-check:** if a sentence's meaning would change were the founding nation swapped for a different one, that sentence violates this rule.

**The scale of time that makes this rule necessary, not just a style preference:** the Falkland Treaty was signed **June 21, 2564.** William Shakespeare was born April 23, 1564. **We are, today, closer to the birth of William Shakespeare than we are to the signing of the Falkland Treaty** — and closer to the publication of the King James Bible (1611) too. Expecting a Tepenian city's present-day culture to still resemble its founding nation's real-world stereotypes is exactly as implausible as expecting modern English culture to still resemble Shakespeare's England just because Shakespeare was English. That much elapsed time makes a founding nation pure geography — a GPS coordinate marking where something started, never a live causal force on what a culture looks like on the far side of that gap.

**Sharper still, confirmed 2026-07-24: the founding generation itself had zero living connection, not just present-day Tepenians.** Real-world Antarctic research stations mostly date to the International Geophysical Year (1957-58); even the oldest continuously-run station (Argentina's Orcadas, 1904) is still roughly 600-660 years short of the Falkland Treaty. **The station's real-world national origin was already ancient history on day one of Tepenia's own existence** — the 2564 exiles who actually signed the treaty had no living connection to whichever national team built the station they arrived at, before a single day of independent Tepenian culture had happened yet. **Direct consequence:** a city's own founding-history narrative should never read as if the real-world station's construction and Tepenia's founding happened in causal or temporal proximity. This rules out more than culture-as-inheritance: it rules out **real station personnel from the 1950s-70s still being alive, present, or personally interacting with the 2564 founding exiles** — a distinct, more literal violation than a stereotype.

**Refined 2026-07-25 — the mechanism for *most* stations is rotating hand-off maintenance across the First Interwar Period (2083-2564), not centuries of dormancy.** An earlier framing ("sat dormant, unstaffed, for centuries") solved the institutional-continuity problem but created a physical-plausibility one — several founding cities' own identities depend on inheriting genuinely *functional*, well-maintained infrastructure, which a centuries-derelict shell doesn't support. **The actual mechanism:** most stations were continuously maintained by a rotating succession of different national operators, handed off from one to another over five centuries — **which specific countries held a given station at any point in that chain is explicitly irrelevant and should never be specified.** This keeps GPS-purposes-only fully intact (no single institution's culture, personnel, or continuity persists — the point is that it changed hands repeatedly) while explaining why the infrastructure was still functional when the 2564 exiles arrived. **Avoid a circular trap:** never have the rotation conveniently end back with the station's original nation — if the chain is genuinely unspecified and insignificant, that convenient ending does no real work and isn't a coincidence worth writing. The rotation's only job is explaining functional infrastructure across the gap — **never** re-deriving which nation a city's founding population turns out to be (that's established separately, per city, on unrelated grounds).

**Two deliberate exceptions, not defaults:**
- **Byrd** actually fell out of the maintenance chain and was abandoned — exactly why it's rare enough to be discoverable much later, distinct from the normal (maintained-throughout) case every other founding city follows.
- **Zhongshan** — given its already-established exclusive, single-nation Jeju-do claim — plausibly stayed under continuous Chinese/Sinian habitation throughout the First Interwar Period rather than passing through rotating operators. This is generational *habitation continuity* (a population, naturally evolving over five centuries), not the *institutional-culture* continuity the rule forbids, and it reinforces rather than substitutes for the Jeju-do ruling. Only reach for an exception like this where a city already has its own separate, established reason to be a special case.

**Preserved records, not personhood, can still cross the gap.** Whether a station passed through rotating operators or (Zhongshan's case) one continuous population, journals, logs, and orientation manuals left behind are artifacts, not living institutions — exiles can plausibly find and learn from preserved documentary records without that being taught by a living institution or person. Learning from a centuries-old written record is categorically different from being personally instructed by surviving personnel, and doesn't violate this rule the way the latter would.

Any inherited operational skill or discipline in a founding-era story should be sourced from **ex-program exiles among the founding population itself** — real people with real 20th/21st-century institutional backgrounds, exiled in 2564, who bring genuine expertise to infrastructure that outside operators kept running — never from the original station's own personnel bridging a 600-plus-year gap.

## Every way this has been violated before (and corrected, in InnerTepeniaGDD)

1. Explaining a city's present-day culture or a faction's internal conflict via founding-nation stereotype ("German rigor," "Russian dusha") instead of the city's own independently-developed identity.
2. A bare "Being [Nationality]." or "[Nationality] Tepenian city" identity claim in a summary line.
3. Picking, rejecting, or even neutrally mentioning whether a real-world creative-inspiration pick shares a nationality with a city's founding nation — even a defensive "no overlap, no issue" note is itself a violation.
4. Framing an established in-world cultural practice (an institution, cuisine, music, art) as inherited from or carried over from the founding real-world nation, rather than developed independently by the exile community. A practice may echo a real-world tradition as coincidence; it must never be explained by that tradition.
5. Using a city's founding nation as an explanatory factor in an in-world strategic, military, or narrative decision (e.g. "Upper Earth struck City X, not City Y, because X's founding nation gave it cleaner symbolic legibility as a target").
6. Writing a city's founding-history narrative as if the real-world station's original national personnel, command structure, or institutional continuity survived the 600-plus-year gap to the 2564 exiles — e.g. describing an "Air Force institutional character from the founding era" as if it passed down from the actual 1950s-70s Air Force rather than being independently re-established by ex-Air Force exiles among the 2564 arrivals themselves.

## How to apply

- The founding nation can still appear as a bare fact: demographic tier tables, "the station was founded by X in 19XX," a naming choice honoring a specific historical figure. It just can never be the *reason* something else in the fiction is true.
- When writing a city's culture, institutions, cuisine, music, or art, build it from the exile community's own circumstances (isolation, density, terrain, founding-era pressures) — never from the founding nation's real-world culture.
- When choosing real-world creative inspirations for a city, judge them entirely on structural/thematic parallel — never mention whether they share or don't share a nationality with the city's founding nation, in either direction (even a defensive disclaimer that there's no overlap is itself the mistake).
- When explaining a plot event (why a strike hit one city and not another, why a faction formed where it did), build the explanation from the city's own established facts — population, geography, infrastructure, civic role, timeline — never from its founding nation.
- When writing a city's founding-era history, infrastructure survives to 2564 via a rotating, unspecified succession of national operators (never name or dwell on which nations) — or, for Zhongshan specifically, continuous single-population habitation. Either way, it can never hand the exiles institutional knowledge, personnel, or command continuity. If a founding story needs someone with real operational expertise, source it from **ex-program exiles among the 2564 founding population** (real people with real prior institutional backgrounds, themselves exiled) — preserved written records (logs, manuals) can also cross the gap as artifacts, just not living instruction.

## Case study: Juan Carlos

Juan Carlos (Palmer subnet) was founded on a pre-exile Spanish research station. Its own source material once framed its defining civic institution (a "tertulia") and its cuisine/music/arts as directly inherited from Spain — corrected in InnerTepeniaGDD to note any such institution would have to be independently developed by the exile community, with real-world echoes noted only as coincidence, never as cause. This repo's own Juan Carlos entry (`Worldspace/Locations/Juan_Carlos.md`) goes a step further and omits the unconfirmed cultural institution entirely rather than porting it over in reframed form, pending real independent development.
