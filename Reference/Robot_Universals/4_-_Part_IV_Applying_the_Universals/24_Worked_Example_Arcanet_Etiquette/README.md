# Chapter 24: Worked Example: Arcanet Etiquette

This chapter is a slightly different kind of worked example than the others in this Part. It is not a
concrete instance of a robot universal — it is a concrete instance of the differentiation engine (Chapter
18) operating on a piece of shared infrastructure that carries no cultural content of its own until a given
population actually starts using it.

## The Arcanet as Plain Infrastructure, Not a Culture Unto Itself

The Arcanet is, functionally, the Antarctican Internet: a regional data and communications network,
nothing more, and nothing mystical. It does not itself constitute a culture, a belief system, or an
identity. Left on its own, it is simply infrastructure — a channel capable of carrying whatever a given
population actually chooses to move through it. Any cultural weight the Arcanet ends up carrying comes
entirely from how populations actually use it, not from anything inherent to the network itself.

## Population-Specific Norms of Use

Because the Arcanet itself carries no built-in cultural content, how any given population actually uses it
is exactly the kind of substantive material the differentiation engine is built to explain: condition — a
population's own economic character, political history, and social values — produces variation in how the
same underlying infrastructure actually gets used. A population oriented around practical survival uses it
one way. A population with a history of institutionalized duplicity uses it another way. A population
almost entirely cut off from it develops a relationship to information itself that looks nothing like
either.

## Worked Instances Already in Canon

Fort McMurdo's own Arcanet use is overwhelmingly practical: shift logs, equipment status reports, safety
documentation, extraction-crew coordination. Social and gossip use of the network is comparatively thin
there — the Arcanet functions as a tool for keeping the work running, not a space for cultural or personal
expression.

Janbogo's own subnet Arcanet use is similarly practically oriented — wind warnings, supply coordination,
community check-ins — and comparatively light on social broadcasting or archival work. This tracks a
culture that handles care and community awareness through direct, in-person hosting rather than mediated
communication, not a limitation of the network itself.

The tech district in Concordia's own Twin Channels are the most elaborate worked instance of this pattern
in canon: an official public Arcanet running alongside an unofficial shadow network carrying different
traffic, frequently maintained by the same people on different shifts. The duality is so fundamental to the
district's own operation that it has become the district's defining character — a case where
population-specific use of a single piece of infrastructure produced not just a habit, but an entire civic
identity built on top of it.

Dome Fuji sits at the opposite extreme: near-total absence of Arcanet connectivity, a consequence of its own
extreme-altitude isolation. Every devotee there is, functionally, a time capsule of their home city at the
exact moment they left it, since there is no incoming Arcanet feed to update that picture and word-of-mouth
between devotees is the only channel left. Dome Fuji's population does not share a collective "present" the
way every other Tepenian city does — the clearest possible demonstration that the Arcanet's cultural
significance comes entirely from access to it, not from any property of the network standing apart from the
populations that do or do not use it.
