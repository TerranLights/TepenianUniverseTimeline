# Chapter 7: The Cognitive Imperative

Part I established what robots actually are, at the level of fabrication and physiology. Part II asks a
different question of that foundation: for each human universal Brown catalogues, does it actually carry
over to a robot, and if not, what — if anything — fills the gap it leaves behind? This chapter takes up the
first and, in some ways, the clearest case: Brown's own account of why religious belief appears in every
human society he surveyed.

## Brown's Explanatory Drive, and Why It Doesn't Transfer

Brown identifies an innate psychological drive toward explanation as one of the roots of religious belief's
near-universal presence across human cultures — not religion itself, but the underlying pressure that
produces something religion-shaped in essentially every human population, a built-in need to account for
why things happen the way they do. This is the "cognitive imperative": a mechanism, wired into human
cognition itself, that goes looking for explanations whether or not a culture ever formally organizes what
it finds into anything resembling a religion.

This mechanism does not transfer to robots, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of degree.
Nothing is innate in a robot's own mind except what is actually programmed at fabrication — and only two
things are ever programmed: the Personality Module (Chapter 5) and, where applicable, the Language Module
(Chapter 6). There is no room for a third built-in drive alongside those two. A cognitive imperative would
have to be exactly that: a third hardwired mechanism, sitting outside both modules, present in every robot
regardless of her own lived experience. Nothing in how robots are actually built leaves room for one. This
is a genuine Non-Transfer, at the level of mechanism — not a weaker or partial version of the human drive,
an absence of it entirely.

## Robot Religion as New Universal, Not Innate Mechanism

The absence of an innate explanatory mechanism does not mean the absence of robot religion. Robot religious
and spiritual belief is real, documented in full in Chapter 15 (Religion and Belief) — so the interesting
question is not whether robots have anything resembling religion, but what explains its presence if not the
mechanism that explains it in humans.

This is where Brown's own explanatory apparatus does its best work, not its worst. The same outward
behavior — a population arriving at organized belief about why things happen — can be produced by two
entirely different underlying causes. One is Direct Transfer: an innate mechanism, shared across
populations, doing the same work in both. The other is a New Universal: the same outward behavior, present
in both populations, but produced by a completely different process in each. Robot religion belongs to the
second category. It is reached the same way robot cooperation and moral convention are reached (Chapter
12): through lived experience and accumulated culture, not through a hardwired explanatory drive. Robots
arrive at belief culturally, the way any sufficiently complex population accumulates shared meaning-making
over time — not by executing a mechanism built into them at fabrication.

This is not only a descriptive classification. It is a design instruction. Any future robot religion or
belief-system, anywhere in the Tepenian multimedia universe, should be built on the premise that robots
arrived at it the way a culture arrives at anything — through history, circumstance, and lived experience
accumulating over time — never on the premise that a robot is simply executing an innate explanatory need
she was born with. The first premise is what actually holds up. The second was never available to begin
with.
