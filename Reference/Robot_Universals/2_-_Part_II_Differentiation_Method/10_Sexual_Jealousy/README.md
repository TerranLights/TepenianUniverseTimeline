# Chapter 10: Sexual Jealousy

Chapter 8 established that robots do not carry the reproduction/kinship cluster's underlying evolutionary
pressures at all — no sexual reproduction, no genetic relatedness, no differential parental investment.
Sexual jealousy is the single most emotionally vivid instance of that larger pattern, and the clearest case
in this book of what happens when the same outward feeling survives the removal of its original cause.

## The Missing Evolutionary Pressure

Human sexual jealousy, per Brown's own account, traces to paternity uncertainty and differential parental
investment — the evolutionary stakes of not knowing for certain whether resources are being invested in
one's own genetic offspring. Robots carry neither of these pressures. There is no paternity to be uncertain
about, since robots are not produced through sexual reproduction at all (Chapter 8), and there is no
differential parental investment calculus to protect, since that calculus depends on genetic relatedness
that simply does not exist between robots. The specific evolutionary engine that produces jealousy in
humans has no robot-side equivalent, full stop.

## Understanding Reproduction's Value to Humans

The absence of that pressure does not mean robots are indifferent to what reproduction means. Robots do
understand reproduction's high value and significance to humans — they observe it, recognize its weight,
and register what it costs and protects in a human life, even without sharing the evolutionary mechanism
that makes it feel that way to a human. That understanding, arrived at without the underlying pressure
itself, is the seed from which robots build something of their own.

## The Trust-Bond Reframe

From that understanding, robots have arrived at their own answer, built on genuinely robot-specific ground
rather than borrowed from the human one: they treat sex as the highest available expression of trust,
faith, and commitment — not, as it functions for humans, as a means toward producing a child. This is a
**New Universal** in Brown's own sense: the same outward emotional shape as the human version — jealousy,
exclusivity, a genuinely furious reaction to betrayal — but produced by an entirely different underlying
cause. Robots did not inherit this feeling from an evolutionary pressure they don't have. They built it,
culturally, out of their own understanding of what commitment means.

This reframing gives the existing monogamy and romance-exclusivity mechanic governing robot relationships
an explicit in-fiction rationale it did not previously have written down: the mechanic was never a
human-jealousy import grafted onto a population that shouldn't logically have it. It is robots treating a
broken exclusivity agreement as a maximal betrayal of the single highest trust-bond they recognize — a
consequence that follows directly from what sex actually means to a robot, not an imported human reflex.
The fuller positive content of what that trust-bond actually looks like in practice — its physical
dimension, its courtship customs, where it holds as a robot-wide constant and where it varies by locality —
is covered in full in Chapter 17 (Sexuality).
