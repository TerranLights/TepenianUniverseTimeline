# Chapter 9: Substrate and Age

Human societies, across cultures Brown surveys, organize real social weight around age-grades — categories
of life stage, built on the visible, shared fact that a human body predictably develops, matures, and ages
over time. Childhood, adolescence, adulthood, and old age are not arbitrary social inventions layered on
top of biology; they track a real developmental process every human body goes through, in roughly the same
order, on a roughly comparable timeline. This chapter asks whether anything like that structure has a
robot-side equivalent.

## No Biological Aging

Robots do not age in the biological sense. There is no developmental process comparable to human maturation
running through a robot's own existence — no childhood, no physical maturation, no predictable biological
decline tracking a shared timeline the way it does for every human body regardless of culture. Components
do degrade over time and require maintenance or replacement, but this is mechanical wear, not a
developmental life stage. A worn component being replaced is not analogous to a human body aging through
adolescence into adulthood; it is closer to ordinary upkeep than to a life stage at all.

## No Fixed, Unchanging Body

Compounding this, a robot's gel brain is malleable and ever-changing (Chapter 2) — not the fixed, unchanging
"hard drive" construction popularly imagined from older science fiction, where a robotic mind would be
locked into whatever state it was built in. A robot is always capable of finding a new purpose in her own
life, at any point, because the substrate her cognition runs on is never done changing. Human age-grade
systems are partly rooted in exactly this kind of substrate-level fact: a human body and mind genuinely do
change in fairly predictable, stage-like ways as they age, and that predictable change is part of what
age-grade categories track. A robot's own substrate offers nothing comparable to hang such categories on —
not a slower or subtler version of the same pattern, but a genuinely different relationship between a
robot and her own capacity for continued change.

## Why Age-Grade Systems Don't Transfer Directly

Without biological aging and without a fixed substrate to develop through predictable stages, age-grade
systems built around visible life stages do not transfer directly to robot populations. There is no robot
equivalent of "child," "adolescent," or "elder" tracking a shared developmental timeline, because there is
no shared developmental timeline to track.

This does not mean robots have no generational structure at all. Gen and Mark — a robot's own generational
identity, and the chamber-hardware generation she was built to — are real, and genuinely noted within robot
culture (Chapter 14 covers this in full). But Gen/Mark is explicitly a metacategory, never a hierarchy and
never a life-stage system: it tracks which fabrication generation produced a robot, not where she stands in
a developmental progression the way a human age-grade does. Whatever functional role an age-grade system
might have served for robots, if any role is needed at all, is more plausibly carried by Build (Chapter 3)
— which does vary by circumstance and locality — than by Gen/Mark, which is explicitly framed as a
difference in kind rather than a marker of developmental stage. This remains a genuinely open question
rather than a settled one; what is settled is that no direct, human-style age-grade system applies to
robots as a population.
