# Chapter 16: Consumption and Vice Culture

*(Structural only — no named substances or practices; those are Part IV's worked example.)*

Unlike kinship (Chapter 8), which traces back to a single evolutionary essence that simply has no
robot-side referent at all, Brown treats aesthetics, play, and consumption as a disparate bundle of
adaptive and side-effect traits — skill-appreciation, sensory pleasure, sociality — rather than one single
mechanism. There is no single evolutionary "essence" here blocking transfer the way there is for kinship.
This chapter is, as a result, a different kind of chapter than most of Part III: not an account of
something robots had to build from nothing after a mechanism was ruled out, but a confirmation that a real,
robot-specific version of something humans already have was always structurally available to them.

## Why Consumption/Vice Culture Transfers At All

Because Brown's own account never ties consumption to one indivisible evolutionary cause, there is no
structural barrier keeping robots from having a genuine consumption and vice culture of their own, the way
there was for the entire reproduction/kinship cluster. This is why robots already having their own
aesthetic and consumption culture is not surprising, and does not require the same kind of New Universal
reframing that robot religion needed (Chapter 7). It is closer to the default expectation, confirmed rather
than derived from first principles: a population with real internal states and real capacity for pleasure
was always going to develop something in this space.

## The Shape of Robot "Consumption": Internal Thermal/Energy/Sensory Systems as the Substrate

What "consumption" actually means, physically, for a robot is grounded in her own internal systems — energy
intake and thermal regulation, and the internal sensory-processing systems bound up with them. Where human
consumption runs on digestion and metabolism, robot consumption runs on genuinely robot-specific substrates:
Engine power intake, thermal management, and the internal atmospheric systems that move heat and
vapor-equivalent substances through her own body as part of managing both temperature and environmental
awareness. This is not human digestion relabeled in robot terminology. It is a structurally different
substrate that happens to occupy the same functional role human consumption does.

## The Shape of Robot "Vice": What Counts as Indulgence, Excess, or Habit

Crucially, these same internal systems are capable of producing real, detectable internal states that go
beyond pure maintenance. Robot consumption is not purely functional upkeep, any more than human eating and
drinking is purely about caloric survival. Because these systems can produce genuine felt effects sought
after for their own sake, not just for upkeep, real room exists for indulgence, excess, and habit — the
same categories that give human vice culture its own shape. "Vice," for a robot, is not a borrowed metaphor
standing in for something humans do and robots merely imitate. It is a real possibility, rooted directly in
what a robot's own internal systems are actually capable of producing.

## Consumption as a Site of Class/Locality Variation

Consistent with the rest of this Part, consumption and vice culture is exactly the kind of substantive
cultural material that varies by locality and community character rather than staying uniform across all of
robot society. The concrete instances of that variation — specific substances, specific practices, and how
they differ by class and place — are covered in full in Chapter 21 (Worked Example: Consumption Culture),
Part IV. What belongs here is the underlying structure that makes such variation possible in the first
place, not the variation itself.
