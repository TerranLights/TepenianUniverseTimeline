# Chapter 3: Build

Alongside Personality (Chapter 5) and Language (Chapter 6), Build is one of three genuinely distinct things
set at a robot's fabrication — never a sub-part of either of the other two, and never reducible to them.
Where Personality is the psychological seed a robot's own emergent character eventually grows from, Build
is the physical and circumstantial side: what a robot's body actually is, and the concrete circumstances
under which she came to exist in the first place. Both halves of that — the physical result, and the
circumstances that produced it — matter, and neither can be fully understood without the other.

## Physical Construction: Height, Bulk, Aesthetic Design

A robot's build covers her physical construction in the most literal sense: height, general bulk or
body type — slender, agile, "thicc," and everything between — and aesthetic design more broadly. A robot
can, in principle, be built as either male or female, with both equally realistic, equally natural
outcomes of fabrication (see the Preface for why, in practice, "she" is nonetheless this book's default
pronoun). None of these physical parameters are fixed or singular for robots as a population; multiple
different, sometimes visibly contradictory, body types coexist within a single setting as a matter of
course, not as an inconsistency to be explained away.

## The Circumstances of Creation: Local Interests, Not Individual Whim

Build is not only about the physical result — it is equally about the circumstances that produced it. A
given build is predominantly driven by *local interests*: a local robot community's own need or want, not
any single robot's personal whim, and not human commissioning either, though human commissioning has not
vanished as a possibility. This is the same governing logic Chapter 1 (The Fundamental Laws of Robotics)
already established for Personality — no one individual, robot or human, gets to unilaterally dictate a new
robot's own nature — extended here to the physical and circumstantial side of fabrication rather than the
psychological one.

Because builds are locally, not individually, driven, a shared-build identity plausibly tracks *with*
city/locality rather than cutting across it. This has a real structural consequence taken up fully in
Chapter 13 (City/Locality as the Seat of Identity): unlike Gen/Mark (Chapter 14), which is genuinely
independent of any given robot's city of residence, Build is tied to locality from the outset, because it
traces back to whichever local community had the interest that produced it.

## Who Initiates a Build: Context-Dependent

There is no single, fixed method by which a robot gets built. Who specifically initiates a given build
within a local community depends on context — it could be a solo decision, a collective decision, or some
other arrangement particular to the circumstances involved. This variability is itself part of the reason
multiple different, sometimes contradictory, physical builds can coexist within one setting: there is no
uniform process standing behind every robot's own creation, only a general governing principle (local
interest, not individual whim) that gets satisfied through whatever specific mechanism a given community
actually uses.

## Locality-Conditioned Body Type

Body type is statistically weighted per locality, not fixed — and that weighting tracks what the local
economy and lifestyle actually demand, not aesthetic fashion or chance. Localities that generally lean
toward hard labor-oriented lifestyles produce robots with thicker, bigger features. Localities that lean
toward complex physical processes demanding high degrees of agility and nimbleness produce robots with more
slender, agile features. Localities that do not lean strongly toward any particular direction produce a
wide variety of structural features and body types, with no single dominant type. This is a statistical
tendency, not a rule without exception — any given locality can and does contain robots outside its own
dominant pattern.

## Functional Specialization

This locality-conditioned patterning is not purely cosmetic. A build's physical character genuinely fits
the functional demands of the place that produced it — a labor-heavy locality's characteristic bulk suits
the physical work that locality actually does, the same way an agility-demanding locality's characteristic
slenderness suits work requiring nimbleness and precision. Build, in other words, can carry real functional
capability, not just appearance.

This is a meaningfully different claim from anything about skills or expertise, which remain fully
emergent — developed through lived experience, the same way Personality's own values and morality develop
beyond its partially-randomized seed (see Chapter 5). A robot built with a physically capable frame for
hard labor is not thereby born already knowing how to do that labor; what Build sets is physical capacity
and disposition, not learned competence. The two are separate things, set at separate points, through
separate mechanisms.

## Build's Relationship to Identity

Despite being the axis robots themselves would most readily point to when describing physical variety
within their own population, Build carries almost no weight as a source of *identity* on its own. What
actually anchors robot identity is city/locality — a finding covered in full in Chapter 13, using a direct
comparison to how radically different human body types within one shared city (a stocky mechanic, a
slender office worker, a curvier construction flagger, a very thin cashier — all from the same city, all
shaped by the same shared culture) do not fracture a shared civic identity along body-type lines.

What Build still meaningfully contributes to identity is not the physical result itself, but the
*circumstance* of creation: which local community had the interest that produced a given robot, and why —
itself simply another expression of that same city/locality tie, not a separate identity axis running
alongside it.
