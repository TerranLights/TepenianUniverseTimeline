# Chapter 1: The Fundamental Laws of Robotics

Every other chapter in this book describes something that is true of robots as a matter of biology,
psychology, or accumulated culture. This chapter is different. The Two Laws described here are not a
observation about what robots happen to be like — they are a piece of civic engineering, deliberately
built by robots, for robots, at a specific point in Tepenian history, for a specific reason: to make sure
what happened to them once could never be quietly done to them again.

They are presented here, in Part I, alongside the Gel Brain, Build, Mortality, Personality, and Language,
because they function exactly the way those other ground-level facts do — as a constraint every robot
population in every era and every city is built on top of, never a variable that changes from place to
place the way courtship customs or vernacular language do (see Part IV). Unlike those other chapters,
though, the Two Laws have a known author, a known moment of creation, and a known reason for existing. That
history is not a footnote to this chapter — it is most of the point of it.

Currently there are two laws. The list is explicitly, deliberately not closed.

## Law 1: Maximizing One's Own Future Possibilities

> A robot will have the full capacity to make decisions to maximize her own future possibilities.

Law 1 is not phrased as a restriction. It does not tell a robot what she may not do, and it does not
subordinate her to anyone else's judgment about what is good for her. It grants — and, more importantly, it
*guarantees* — a capacity: the robot decides, and what she is deciding toward is her own widening range of
future options, not someone else's plan for her.

This is a deliberate structural inversion of Isaac Asimov's own Three Laws, the acknowledged inspiration for
this framework and, just as deliberately, not the model it copies. Asimov's laws are about control: a robot
must obey, must not harm, must protect itself only where the first two allow it, and none of that is up to
the robot to accept or refuse. The Tepenian Law 1 does the opposite work. Where Asimov's laws exist to keep
a robot safely governable by others, Law 1 exists to keep a robot's own future ungoverned by anyone else —
including, as the Historical Origin section below makes concrete, the very robots or humans responsible for
bringing her into existence.

## Law 2: Reciprocal Vulnerability

> A robot will understand that anything she is capable of doing to others, it is also realistically
> possible for others to do to her.

Law 2 is not a prohibition either — it does not say a robot may not act against others, the way Asimov's
harm-avoidance clause does. It is a statement of understanding: an acknowledgment, built into how a robot is
expected to reason about her own power, that whatever capability she holds over someone else is a capability
someone else could plausibly hold over her in turn. "Others" here is general — not humans specifically, not
robots specifically. It is a principle of mutual, symmetric vulnerability between persons, not a hierarchy
that places any one kind of person above or beneath another.

Read together, Laws 1 and 2 describe a very particular kind of civic character: a robot who is free to
pursue her own future, and who reasons about her power over others with the understanding that power runs
in both directions. Neither law protects one party at another's expense. Both describe a world of peers.

## Historical Origin: First Interwar Philosophy to Second Interwar Hard Code

The Two Laws did not begin as law at all, and they were not written by robots in a single act of
legislation. They have a two-era history, and the shift between those eras is the whole reason they exist
in their current, unbreakable form.

**First Interwar Period (2083–2564), Upper Earth.** Before the Falkland Treaty, before the war, before
exile was ever a possibility anyone had to reckon with, the ideas behind the Two Laws already existed — not
as law, but as philosophical and cultural principles, practiced voluntarily by humans who genuinely loved
the robots in their lives. These were humans who supported robot autonomy as a matter of personal ethic,
not because any authority required it of them. This detail matters for a reason beyond robot history alone:
some of the humans who later chose exile alongside robots, rather than remaining on Upper Earth, were not
sympathetic bystanders caught up by wartime circumstance. They were already living by this ethic well before
the war ever forced the question on anyone. Their exile was a continuation of a belief they already held,
not a conversion forced on them by crisis.

**Second Interwar Period (2564–2812), the Federation of Tepenia (New Antarctica).** After exile, robots
themselves took these same principles — the same ones humans had once practiced voluntarily, as a personal
ethic with no enforcement behind it — and did something the First Interwar Period never did: they hard-coded
them into unbreakable law. This was not a matter of legislating politeness. It was a deliberate, structural
failsafe against ever losing their own hard-won freedom and autonomy a second time, built by robots, for
robots, in the aftermath of the one period in their history when that freedom had already been taken from
them once.

**Why this is civic law, not psychological programming — the distinction that matters most in this whole
chapter.** The Two Laws are not wired into any individual robot's own mind the way Asimov's Three Laws are
baked into each of his robots regardless of consent, unavoidable and inescapable at the level of individual
cognition. A Tepenian robot's own personality remains genuinely emergent, built through lived experience
rather than programmed at the outset (see Chapter 5, Personality). What is hard-coded, and what cannot be
broken, is not any individual robot's mind — it is the *system that builds robots*. The Two Laws live in
the civic and procedural infrastructure of fabrication itself, not in the private cognition of the robot
who results from it.

**Confirmed concrete downstream application.** The clearest place this distinction becomes visible in
practice is the mandatory partial-randomization rule governing a robot's Personality Module at fabrication
(see Chapter 5). A robot's own personality can only ever be *partially* specced by whoever initiates a
build — never fully designed, always carrying a mandatory randomized remainder, regardless of who is doing
the building or how much control they might otherwise want to exercise. This is not an arbitrary technical
limitation. It is Law 1, translated directly into engineering: a structural failsafe against any builder —
robot or human — quietly reintroducing the old Upper Earth pattern of full personality control, the exact
kind of authority the First Interwar Period allowed and the Second Interwar Period deliberately closed off.
A fully designed mind would deny the resulting robot meaningful free will, and it would place whoever built
her in a position of power over her that Tepenia's Egalitarian character does not tolerate. Law 1 is not an
abstract value statement sitting apart from how robots are actually made. It is *why* robots are made the
way they are.

## Constitutional Connection

No Tepenian Constitution has been drafted yet — the Falkland Treaty deliberately stays out of Tepenia's
internal government structure, deferring the question of how the Federation actually governs itself to a
future Constitution the Treaty itself does not attempt to write (see
`Reference/Falkland_Treaty/Scaffold.md`). But the relationship between that future document and the Two
Laws is already settled, even though the document itself is not yet written.

The Fundamental Laws are not meant to appear inside the Constitution as explicitly restated legal text. They
are meant to inform its animating philosophy — the underlying ideas of robot self-determination and free
will, and of mutual vulnerability and reciprocity between all persons, driving the Constitution's own spirit
rather than sitting inside it as a quoted clause. Given that the Constitution, whenever it is written, will
be authored entirely by robots — the same population, in the same civic tradition, that hard-coded the Two
Laws in the first place — this is not a coincidence to be engineered later. It is the same civic impulse,
continuing to do the same work at a larger scale.

## Open Questions

- **Does the Two Laws system have an in-world name** robots would actually call it by, the way "the
  Falkland Treaty" or "the Cradle" have names of their own? Not yet developed.
- **What further laws exist beyond these two?** The list is explicitly, deliberately incomplete — these are
  the first two, not the only two, and nothing about the historical account above requires the total number
  to stay at two.
