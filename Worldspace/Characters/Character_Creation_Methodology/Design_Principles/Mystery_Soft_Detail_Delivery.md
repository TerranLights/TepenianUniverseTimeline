# Design Principle — Mystery as Soft-Detail Delivery

**What this is:** a refinement of a classic writing-craft caution, sharpened for production use across a large
cast. See `../Pipeline/02_Information_Processing.md`'s "Mystery Caution" governing principle for where this
sits inside the full diagnostic pipeline — this file is the generalized, standalone version of that same rule.

---

## The Classic Version, and Why It's the Wrong Shape for Production

The traditional craft advice is that a character shouldn't be fully resolved even by the writer — that full
transparency kills what makes her worth continued attention. This sounds appealing but doesn't hold up well at
production scale: it leaves the writer genuinely unsure of things that should be knowable, and it conflates
two different questions — *does the writer know this* and *does the audience get told this directly*.

## The Refined Rule

**Full psychological resolution belongs entirely in the private design/character notes. Nothing about a
character's psychology needs to stay genuinely unknown to whoever's building her.**

What actually stays withheld is specifically what surfaces *in the finished media itself* — a fully-defined
detail can be deliberately kept out of direct exposition and dialogue, and instead only gestured at through
soft, ambient details embedded in the world around her:

- What she drinks or eats, and when
- What routines and small habitual gestures she keeps
- What she wears, and how she wears it day to day (beyond any locked visual reference)
- What entertainment or personal effects she keeps in her own space
- A specific way of handling a tool, object, or task that says something without a word of dialogue

**The mystery is a presentation-layer choice, not a design-layer one.** Define everything privately. Deliver
almost none of it directly. Let soft ambient detail carry the weight instead.

## The "Show the Symptom Before the Cause" Technique

A closely related, more concrete technique: introduce a character fully characterized through routine
competence and small habitual gestures — a specific way of maintaining her living space, an offhand physical
tell, a terse verbal habit — before a single word of backstory is spoken. A description of a dwelling or
personal effects can carry the same characterizing weight as a line of dialogue, often more, since it isn't
filtered through anyone's self-presentation.

## Why This Matters More at Scale

For a single character in a single story, the classic "keep some mystery even from yourself" approach can
work fine. For a project building a large cast under one methodology, it doesn't: an author who genuinely
doesn't know a character's own full psychology has no reliable way to keep dozens or hundreds of characters
internally consistent with themselves over time. Full private resolution, partial public delivery, gives you
both consistency *and* the same felt sense of mystery an audience actually experiences — the character reads
as having unplumbed depths because most of what's true about her simply isn't being said, not because it
doesn't exist yet.
